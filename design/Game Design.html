Back on the Block

I want a 2D open-world game that runs in a browser and feels like an actual place someone could live in. The player should be able to walk around, drive, talk to people, work, buy things, get into trouble, and slowly build a life. Crime is a big part of the world, but the whole point is having choices and dealing with what comes after them. I want the player to be able to make a mess of things, realise they have made a mess of things, and still have a way to recover.

The theme is Bounce Back. That should show up in the actual systems as much as the story. Losing money, damaging a friendship, getting injured, losing a job, or getting caught should change what the player does next. There should be a way forward after each of those things. Recovery can take work and sometimes cost something, but a mistake should create another situation to play through. I dont want the player reloading every time the game stops going perfectly.

Back on the Block is set in Port Lucero, a made-up island in the western Atlantic, east of Florida and up near the northern Caribbean. Alex comes back to a place they used to know. Miles Bennett has sorted out a room and thinks there might be work down at dispatch. He means well. That doesn't mean he's checked who signs the pay slips. That's where I want this to start. Some of the stuff later in this document is where I'd like to take it after this build.

I want this document finished before the game is put together. It describes the intended game and the decisions that should guide its implementation. It should be possible to read it later and understand why a feature exists, what the player can do with it, and what should happen when it goes wrong. Any values mentioned here, like a conversation range, a shop price, or the length of a police response, can be adjusted during testing. The player experience and the reasons behind those values matter more than pretending the first numbers will be perfect.

The world should have a modern, slightly worn feel. People still go to work, shops open and close, buses or other traffic move through the streets, and ordinary life carries on around the player. The humour can come from rent, terrible bosses, ridiculous paperwork, cheap advertising, strange side businesses, neighbourhood gossip, and people who take very small amounts of power far too seriously. I want it to feel grounded enough that the absurd parts land. A shopkeeper calmly selling an expensive emergency sandwich while a broken alarm refuses to stop is more interesting than every person acting like a random joke machine.

It should be dark humour without making every serious moment meaningless. Someone can joke while they are under pressure, then get quiet when the consequences reach home. Someone who is funny in a shop can still be scared when threatened. Violence should get a response from the people around it. The city should be able to be funny, selfish, kind, hostile, boring, and unfair at different times. The writing should allow people to disagree with the player without turning them into obvious villains or lectures.

I want the street to look like somewhere people have to live in August. Sun straight off a white roof, green grass in the parks, a line of shade people can actually stand in. The sea can be properly blue. People should come in different heights and builds, wear different clothes and walk like they have somewhere to be. A car needs room in its lane and a person should fit through their own front door. A little dust off a wheel or an ambulance light catching the road tells you alot more than a colour filter over the whole town.

The image and audio asset rule is firm. No AI-generated visual or audio assets should be used. Existing supplied artwork can be used where it fits and where its source and permissions are known. Ordinary image processing is fine, including cropping, reducing resolution, limiting colours, aligning animation frames, and scaling with nearest-neighbour filtering. Those changes should be reproducible and preserve the original files. Procedural drawing in the game is also fine. Calling a generated image pixel art after lowering its resolution would not satisfy this requirement.

The music will come from tracks I provide. The audio system should let those tracks be assigned to the title screen, ordinary exploration, story moments, or pursuit as appropriate. Until those files arrive, the game can be tested with silence or clearly identified temporary sounds. Final claims about the soundtrack should wait until the real tracks are integrated. Volume controls need to work independently for music and effects. Browser audio should start cleanly after a player interaction, and pausing or switching tabs should handle the sound without leaving a loud engine or alarm running unexpectedly.

The opening should give the player a broad reason to be here. The protagonist gets pulled into illegal deliveries, either because someone disguises the work as something harmless or because the player knowingly accepts a risky opportunity. Either way can work for the opening. The introduction should make the circumstances clear enough that the player's choice feels informed where it is meant to be informed. If a delivery is a deception, later revelations should come from that deception. If the player takes the job knowingly, the story should remember that rather than rewriting them as an innocent victim.

I like the protagonist having a real, fixed backstory. They existed before the first moment of play. The details should be chosen once and remain consistent across every route, conversation, and save. The player can decide how to talk about that history and how to behave now. They should not accidentally create contradictory childhoods or past relationships by picking different dialogue options. The starting backstory might involve financial pressure, a broken opportunity, or returning to a neighbourhood after things went wrong, but those specifics are proposals until they are agreed.

The protagonist should want a way out of the life they are getting dragged into. Building something better is the long-term pull. That could mean stable work, a business, a home they can keep, a repaired relationship, or simply getting enough control over their own time that someone else stops choosing their future. The game should leave room for the player to define what a better life looks like through their actions. A person who earns an honest wage and keeps a small circle of friends should have a valid route through the game.

The story should have three flexible phases. The first is getting established and becoming entangled. The player learns the neighbourhood, meets people, discovers opportunities, and gets a clearer picture of the delivery work. The second is pressure and response. The player has more relationships and options, but their choices start producing larger obligations and consequences. The third is finding a way out or deciding what they are willing to live with. These phases should guide the story's pace while allowing the player to keep exploring and making their own decisions.

A phase should advance because something meaningful happened, rather than because the player walked through one invisible checkpoint. For example, discovering what a package contains, refusing another job, earning enough to settle an obligation, helping a person affected by a delivery, or bringing useful evidence to someone could all move the story forward. The implementation needs explicit conditions for each route so the flexibility is real. The same phase can have different entry points and different scenes depending on what the player has already done.

I dont want a story that quietly forces crime after promising freedom. There can be pressure, threats, temptation, and advantages to taking illegal work. There should also be lawful routes that remain playable. A refusal might cost the player quick money or make a contact less friendly, then open a slower route through ordinary work or another person's help. The player should not discover that every honest option is just a dead end and the only actual mission is to do the crime anyway.

The ending should resolve the main pressure without deleting the world. Once the broad story has been resolved, the player should be able to carry on living there. Their money, home situation, relationships, reputation, and unresolved local problems should still matter. Different endings can change the available contacts or the way people respond, while leaving ordinary work, shopping, exploring, driving, and small events available. The city needs something worth doing after the final story conversation, even if the pace becomes quieter.

Port Lucero stays in the same place every time you start. The friend's front door, the clinic, the tower downtown and the road along the water should be things I can recognise and give someone directions to. If I tell my friend to meet me outside the garage, they should know which corner I mean when they play their own game. What changes is who just got off shift, which street is busy and what we get mixed up in on the way.

Bellport, North Marina, Coral Hill and South Quay are the four parts of the island you can walk around. A tall building is probaby a better landmark than another sign telling me where I am. I like the idea of being able to see a tower over the lower roofs, then working out how close I am as the streets change. A shop can be two doors from a block of flats. People need their shade, their courtyards and their bit of pavement even when the buildings get big.

Terrain needs to affect the layout in a readable way. A waterfront, a hill, a park, rail land, or an industrial edge can give the city structure and make routes feel different. The streets should keep important places reachable on foot, with a car providing convenience and new options. Main roads can connect districts, smaller streets can create local shortcuts, and pedestrian paths can reward learning the neighbourhood. The player should be able to form a mental map instead of relying on an arrow for every trip.

Important locations should have a clear purpose. The starting home needs somewhere to recover and manage the day. Shops need useful stock. Workplaces need opening times and a way to start a shift. A clinic or other health service needs to be reachable after an injury. Police facilities should support surrender, release, or resolving certain obligations. Social places need enough activity for relationships to form. Optional interiors can add flavour, but the first completed version should prioritise places that support actual play.

Building interiors should make sense with their exteriors. A small corner shop should not contain a huge warehouse unless the building establishes that space. Each entrance needs a stable destination and return position. Rooms should have walkable routes, interactable counters, and furniture that fits their use. The room layout should leave space for the player and NPCs around doors, counters, seats, and work points. Decorative detail should fill the spaces left after those practical needs are met.

Every door that looks usable needs a clear response. It can open, be locked, have posted hours, belong to someone who refuses entry, or simply be marked as private. The response should stop the player from wondering whether the game is broken. A shop that closes at night should visibly close, with stock and staff remaining consistent when it opens again. Interiors should preserve things like a broken object, a purchased item, or an event's aftermath when those changes matter to later play.

The environment should carry detail through use. A delivery office can have stacked parcels, a flickering number display, a desk with the wrong opening times, and a notice about late arrivals while the manager arrives late. A residential hall can have different doors, rubbish collection reminders, a neighbour's plant, and an argument audible behind one room. These details should help people understand where they are. They should also leave enough contrast and clear floor space that the player can see exits and interactions.

If I come back tomorrow, Elena Cruz should still be the cycle courier who keeps the stamps from letters she couldn't deliver. Miles is the marine electrician who knows the garage. Alma Reyes has spent years hemming uniforms around here. People speak mostly English and Spanish on the island. There are also Indian, Pakistani, Turkish and other families who've made a home here. A name, a visible detail, a place they actually go and something they care about make a better introduction than a wall of biography.

People have a home address, a shift, a break and a day off. Two people who share a flat can leave for work hours apart. Someone can be at the morning market while another resident is still at home. I want to run into the same person on the way out, see them at their job later, then hear them talking about something entirely different when they're off. The minute an accident happens, their neat little schedule should have to wait.

A schedule should not make a person behave stupidly when something happens. A worker who sees an injury can pause, decide whether to help, and arrive late. A witness may go somewhere safe before making a report. An injured NPC may go to a clinic instead of carrying on to lunch. If a shop closes because its owner is hurt, the shop should show why. The player should be able to understand the disruption and later see the person recover or ask for help.

Not every NPC needs their whole life story on screen. The game can keep a compact profile and reveal the parts the player encounters. A few people can receive more authored detail, while background residents share the same basic systems at lower detail. Important contacts need enough distinct behaviour that the player learns who to trust, who is reliable, who exaggerates, and who changes their mind slowly. A person who has been helpful should remain recognisable even if they are currently annoyed with the player.

NPC money should connect to their situation. Working should earn wages, shopping should cost money, and an injury or missed shift can affect what they can afford. The simulation does not need to account for every coin in the entire city, but money involved in a visible interaction should have a source and a destination. Someone should not buy an unlimited number of objects from the player or lend money endlessly. If they cannot afford to help, they should say so in a way that fits them.

Everyone pays in dollars. If the garage says $25, I want to see that exact amount come out of the same balance I got my wages in. Jobs, sales, rewards, purchases, fees, repairs, and penalties should update one consistent balance and leave a short record the player can inspect. A completed transaction should happen once. Clicking a button twice, pausing at the same moment, or reloading a save should not duplicate a wage or remove the same item twice. The player should know what an offer pays, what conditions it has, and when payment happens before committing to it.

Lawful work should provide actual actions to play through. A shift might involve sorting parcels, cleaning a set of rooms, taking ordinary deliveries, serving customers, repairing equipment, or transporting passengers. Those are examples, with the first jobs chosen for what the game can support well. Work should have clear beginning and end points, a reasonable time cost, and feedback about success. More demanding jobs can open through reliability, a licence, training, or a recommendation. Honest progress needs enough variety and reward to support the main story's exit routes.

Jobs should also be places where the player meets people. Turning up on time can improve a manager's confidence. Covering a shift can help a coworker. Taking responsibility for damaged goods can affect trust differently from hiding it. Doing good work should open opportunities beyond a slightly larger number on the money counter. At the same time, a missed shift should not permanently erase every lawful option. A different employer, an apology, a probationary task, or a few days of reliable attendance can help with that.

Health should have clear causes and recovery options. Violence, traffic collisions, and dangerous events can injure the player. Food or basic recovery items can handle smaller losses, while serious injury can require treatment or rest. The item descriptions should say what they restore and whether they can be used during danger. Health values need limits, and using an item should consume it once. A player who has no money should still have some slower way to recover enough to keep playing.

Shops should sell things that support the world. Food, medical supplies, everyday items, vehicle services, and other useful goods should have believable locations and prices. Shopkeepers should react to opening hours, stock, the player's conduct, and what they personally know. The first version can keep fairly small amounts of stock and restock on a schedule. The economy can get more detailed later. The important part is that buying, selling, stealing, damaging stock, and waiting for a shop to open all follow the same visible rules.

Driving should feel a little different in a van and a smaller car. Steering should ease in, a faster turn should take more road, and if I press reverse while I'm moving forward I expect to slow down first. A turn off the pavement onto grass should be something I can see and feel. Parked cars need to fit the road, moving traffic needs to use its lanes, and when I get out there needs to be an actual clear patch to stand on. Damage should cost something, and the garage should be able to get a car running again.

Vehicles also belong to the social and crime systems. An owner can notice their car is missing and report it. A person who only saw a car should remember that car's description rather than instantly knowing the driver. Someone who already knows the protagonist may recognise them more easily. Parking carelessly might annoy a shopkeeper or block a delivery. A collision should be able to lead to repair costs, an argument, help for an injured person, or a police incident depending on what happened and who saw it.

Guns should exist and have a meaningful role, with ammunition, aiming, firing, reloading, damage, and feedback. The controls should avoid accidental shots while shopping or clicking dialogue. When a menu is open, its clicks belong to that menu. Equipping a weapon should be a deliberate action, and the player should be able to put it away. Ammunition should be tracked consistently. An empty weapon needs a clear response instead of silently failing, and a reload should not create ammunition or consume it twice.

The presence of a gun should change a situation even before it is fired. Nearby people who can see it may become wary, leave, call for help, or refuse service according to the setting's rules and their own temperament. The actual offence rules are fictional game rules that need to be clear to the player. Threatening someone can work in the short term and damage trust or create an investigation later. The world should remember the people affected by the act, rather than treating it as a temporary animation.

Friends should grow out of repeated interactions. A person can appreciate a favour, remember a promise, trust the player with a small responsibility, and eventually offer help of their own. Different friendships should have different values. One friend might help with a job lead, another with a safe place to rest, another with information about a neighbourhood. They should also be able to say no. Being a friend should not mean becoming an unlimited source of money, forgiveness, or free services.

Romance can be an optional result of a relationship if the characters and situation support it. It should never be required for finishing the story or receiving the best practical rewards. Any romantic relationship should involve adult characters, mutual interest, and the ability to decline without being punished by unrelated systems. A person can value the player as a friend without being available romantically. The first version can keep romance small and restrained, with deeper scenes treated as an extension once friendship and trust work properly.

Trust needs to be different for each person. The game should store how much that particular NPC trusts the protagonist and why. Helping one person does not automatically repair a relationship with someone else. A private favour does not become public knowledge by magic. Trust can rise through reliability, help, fair treatment, honesty, and respecting a boundary. It can fall through betrayal, intimidation, theft, broken commitments, or dismissing something important to that person. The amount should depend on the context and their values, thats what matters.

A trust change needs to refer to an event. If the player promises to collect a parcel and actually delivers it, that event can record the promise, the completion, and the NPC's response. If the player admits causing damage and pays for a repair, the person can remember both the harm and the repair. The same action should not award trust endlessly just because the player repeats a conversation. A meaningful first favour, a repeated habit, and an obviously manipulative attempt should be allowed to have different effects.

Trust should not be the only relationship value. Someone can trust the player's competence while disliking them, or like them while refusing to lend money. A compact model can separate trust, familiarity, affection, and fear, with a few remembered facts explaining the current situation. Those exact values are proposed implementation choices. The requirement is that relationships react in a believable, personal way and do not reduce every social situation to one global friendship score.

The protagonist's fixed backstory should come out through those relationships. A stranger should not receive the most personal story just because the player opens a dialogue menu. A trusted person, a private setting, and a relevant conversation can make a disclosure appropriate. Someone helping with an old problem might hear a different part of the same history than a coworker chatting during a shift. The player should be able to decline a personal topic without the game secretly changing the underlying facts.

Dialogue availability should check several things together. The NPC needs to know the player well enough, the topic needs a reason to come up, the person should have time to talk, and the current situation should allow it. A serious conversation should not start while police are chasing the player or a fire is happening in the room. A disclosure should remember that it already happened. Later conversations can build on it and refer to the details that person actually heard, including whether the protagonist was honest or evasive.

Contextual dialogue also means responding to what the player is doing. A person can notice an injury, a missed appointment, a returned item, a recent argument, or the player arriving in a familiar car. They should avoid repeating their introduction every time. The first system can use authored lines selected by conditions and event history. It does not need a language model or unrestricted generated dialogue. Short, specific lines that follow the world state will make a person feel more present than long generic conversations.

Choice should exist outside dialogue. The player can choose a route through town, arrive early or late, bring an item, return something they took, pay an obligation, help someone before leaving, or walk away from a risky situation. Those actions need to be able to change a mission or relationship directly. A contact should notice a completed delivery even if the player chooses a brief response afterwards. The game should not make every moral or strategic decision happen inside a conversation box while actions in the world do nothing.

Moral conduct or honour should be part of the design, with consequences connected to behaviour. A high-level conduct record can help the game recognise habits and select suitable story responses. NPC reactions must still come from what each person saw, experienced, or learned. A player can be privately generous and publicly feared. A harmful act can remain unknown until evidence appears. The system should support those differences, because an all-knowing honour meter would undermine the social world.

I want consequences to be causal enough that the player can explain them. If a worker tells their friend about the player helping them, that information can spread through a relationship. If a theft is unwitnessed and leaves no useful evidence, the owner may know an item is missing without knowing who took it. If the player makes amends directly, the affected person can change their view while someone who heard only the original story stays suspicious. Rumours should have a source, an age, and a degree of certainty.

Memories should be compact records of important events. An NPC can remember the event type, the time, the people involved, the location, whether they experienced it directly, and what they think it means. Strong experiences can last longer than minor ones. People can forget details without forgetting that they were frightened or helped. A memory system needs limits so it stays fast and useful. It should keep significant unresolved promises, harms, debts, disclosures, and relationship changes, while letting small repeated observations merge or expire.

I want one narrow police strip with the time. Blue on the left, red on the right, blending through the middle. Below it I should be able to read what's happening, like a witness calling, an area search or an actual pursuit, with a countdown when there is one. If I've been spotted and someone reaches for a phone, that's a different moment from an officer getting a decent look at me. The strip should make those moments easy to tell apart at a glance.

A report only contains things someone could see. A face up close, the shirt somebody was wearing, a vehicle, a visible weapon, maybe who was walking with them. If nobody saw Alex, there isn't a witness statement that can name Alex. An officer could still respond to a disturbance between two other residents. I should be able to pass by and watch that happen without somehow becoming the suspect because I'm the one with a controller.

A witness has to be close enough, in the right place, with a clear view. A civilian can take a moment to get a call out. There's a chance to speak to them during that window. If Alex tries to scare them out of reporting, one person might back off and another might finish the call anyway. They'll remember being threatened. A report that already got through stays on file. I like that you can make a bad situation worse trying to control it.

Being noticed and being identified are separate things. A person might see a figure running from a damaged shop but not know their face. A patrol may investigate a reported location and find the injured person. A witness may give a description, then later recognise the player in a relevant context. Identification should use the information available to that person and the incident's confidence. The player should get readable feedback about a witnessed act, a report, and a change to immediate danger without being shown every hidden calculation.

Police also need limited knowledge during pursuit. A visible target can be followed. If sight is lost, responding officers can search around a last known location and update it when someone sees the target again. The game should not steer officers through walls using the player's true location. It can keep this manageable with simple patrols, search destinations, and local information sharing. The point is making the pursuit readable and fair, with enough uncertainty that a momentary escape feels earned.

Investigation should continue or resolve for a reason. A case might remain open because a witness identified the player, lose strength when a report has no reliable link, or reach a resolution through surrender, a penalty, restitution, or a story decision. Turning red back to blue should end the immediate chase while preserving whatever the case still knows. The player should be able to inspect a short explanation of their current legal pressure, such as a recent disturbance under investigation or an identified offence awaiting resolution.

Arrest should lead to a recoverable outcome. It can cost time, money, possessions connected to the incident, a job opportunity, or trust. It should return the player to a clear place with enough health and access to keep playing. The exact penalties need balancing so an early mistake is not a permanent economic trap. A lawful job, a support contact, or a basic recovery opportunity should remain available. Any confiscation or payment must be recorded once and survive saving and loading.

The player should have actions besides running or fighting when police arrive. Cooperating, stopping a vehicle, paying a relevant penalty, returning property, or resolving a specific obligation can be options where the fictional rules support them. Officers should give readable instructions and a short chance to comply when the situation allows it. If the player chooses to escalate, the response can escalate. The system should make the reason visible rather than unexpectedly switching every encounter into combat.

I want stuff to happen while I'm doing absolutely nothing about it. Two people talking outside the market, somebody grabbing a bag, a driver hitting a pedestrian. The people involved should stay in the scene. A patrol car can pull up and officers can take someone in. Medics can come in an ambulance, reach the injured person and take them to the clinic. Maybe I'm busy with a delivery and only see the ambulance leaving. Their day still changed while mine went on.

An event chain should be able to grow naturally. Suppose a courier's vehicle breaks down. Helping can earn a small payment and a recommendation for work. Ignoring it can make a shop receive stock late, which annoys the manager and creates an extra task the next morning. Taking the abandoned parcel can give the player goods while creating a missing delivery incident and harming the courier's job. The chain does not need to be enormous. A visible connection between two or three later situations can make the original choice feel real.

Another example is an injury outside a shop. A player who stops and provides a recovery item can lose time on their own shift while gaining the injured person's trust. Calling for help can be useful without using an item. Taking advantage of the distraction can create a separate theft event if someone sees it or if evidence later connects it. If the player drives away, the simulation should still resolve whether another person helps. That keeps the world moving without making every event wait forever for the protagonist.

A social event can work the same way. A friend asks for help moving something before work. Arriving on time, arriving late with an explanation, forgetting, or sending another reliable contact can all lead to different memories. Later, that friend might be willing to cover a shift, introduce someone, or decline another request. The story should avoid counting every delay as betrayal. The NPC's expectations, the seriousness of the promise, and whether the player makes contact should affect the outcome.

Events need pacing. The player should have quiet time to travel, work, shop, or just think for a bit. Occassionally nothing much needs to happen. A new emergency should not spawn every few seconds simply because the event system has nothing else to do. There should be cooldowns by location, person, and event type, with limits on how many urgent situations can be active at once. Major story events should get some space around them. Repeated events should vary in context and recognise that the player has seen something similar before.

Event selection should respect the world. A shop cannot ask for a restock delivery when it is already destroyed or closed for an unrelated reason. Someone who is in hospital cannot be waiting on the pavement for a casual favour. A character who refuses contact should not immediately appear in a friendly conversation event. The event system should reserve the people and locations it needs, check conditions again before starting, and release those reservations if the opportunity expires or becomes impossible.

I want a difference between an invitation and a commitment. An ambient situation can be ignored with ordinary world consequences. Once the player explicitly promises to help, accepts payment, or takes possession of a delivery, the obligation becomes more personal. The UI should show what has been accepted and when it matters. A player should not receive a serious trust penalty for walking past a request they never heard. Missed commitments should refer to a real agreement the player made.

When the game opens I want to see Port Lucero. The harbour, the skyline and the light can do the introducing. Give me New game, Continue if I've got a save, settings and controls. Once I'm out walking I want a small street radar, a clear next lead, the police strip and just enough information to drive or tell how hurt I am. Give the title some attitude and let the actual sentences use a typeface I can read. Theres no reason my notebook needs to be spread across the whole street.

I should hear the end of somebody's conversation as I walk past. Put the name beside the subtitle. If I stop and say something, show my line and their answer the same way. Let me pick a topic without stopping the traffic just so we can talk about lunch. If I leave, the conversation should fall out of earshot. A few small choices by a doorway are enough when there are people, a car and a shop entrance all close together. Pause, inventory, contacts and the full map can have their own screens.

The journal should help the player remember their own decisions. It can show active work, personal commitments, recent relevant information, and known investigation status. It should record what the protagonist actually knows. A confidential disclosure from an NPC should not appear as a public truth everyone shares. A story lead should explain the next possible actions in natural language, including alternative routes where the player has discovered them. The player should have enough guidance to act without the game choosing their route for them.

Accessibility should be designed into the ordinary controls. Important cues need a visual equivalent for sound. Text should have good contrast, dialogue should wait for the player to advance it, and timing should not depend on reading quickly. Reduced motion should disable unnecessary shake, flashes, and heavy screen movement. The player should be able to pause during an ordinary conversation or task. Touch controls can be supported if they are properly tested, but desktop keyboard and mouse play is the sensible first target for the browser release.

The browser has practical limits. The game should load a compact set of assets, keep active simulation local, and avoid making every NPC run full pathfinding every frame. Nearby people and vehicles can use detailed movement, while distant people advance through simpler schedule states. Those distant updates must still preserve important commitments, health, money, and memories. Background detail should be drawn efficiently. A reasonable initial performance target is smooth play around 60 frames per second on an ordinary recent laptop, with a playable lower target if the browser or hardware is slower.

Port Lucero has a fixed street plan and a fixed set of residents. The save needs to keep track of everything that happened to them. That includes the time of day, where people are headed, who is in a vehicle, which people were talking, which bills have been paid and which reports have actually reached the police. A half-finished rescue shouldn't turn back into a fresh accident when I open the game again. Coming back should feel like picking up the same afternoon.

There still needs to be chance in the game. The town looks the same on Monday morning, but somebody with the same route can get a different afternoon. Save the order of upcoming encounters and the parts of a job or emergency that have already happened. Paid wages, shared secrets and witness statements need to survive a reload. The exact curl of dust behind a tyre can be different every time and nobody's going to mind.

Saving needs to be safe at normal stopping points. The game can save after important transactions, commitments, story changes, and periodic quiet moments. It should write a complete new state before replacing the last usable save. If storage is unavailable, full, or denied by the browser, the player needs a clear message so they wont lose track of what has saved. Exporting and importing a save file would be a useful browser feature because local browser storage can be cleared. Thats something I would like to add early if the storage approach supports it.

The game should define what happens while it is closed. My suggestion for the first version is that the world resumes from its saved time. That avoids returning after several real days to find every promise broken and the player's life ruined by events they could not play. Browser tab focus should pause or clearly suspend the simulation. If later versions simulate offline time, that needs to be an explicit feature with fair limits and clear player consent. It should not appear accidentally through the system clock.

Failure should be checked across the whole game. Being broke should leave work or help available. Being injured should leave a recovery route. Losing a vehicle should still allow walking and access to essential locations. Losing one friend should leave other relationships possible. Getting arrested should resolve into a playable situation. A story contact becoming hostile should reveal another route to the same broad phase or a different valid outcome. The game can make recovery difficult without making the save unwinnable because of one local decision.

The consequences still need weight. Recovery should not erase the past immediately. A person can accept repayment but remain cautious. A damaged car can be repaired while the missed delivery still affects work. A resolved case can stop police pressure while an employer remembers the absence. The Bounce Back theme works when the player has to respond to these changes and can eventually build something from them. A reset that makes every mistake disappear would remove the reason to care.

The first complete core should include one familiar island with a day that can be saved and resumed, walking and driving, useful interiors, a small set of jobs and shops, health and recovery, basic weapons, persistent NPCs, personal trust and memories, witness reports and police searches or pursuits, and a playable route through all three story phases. It also needs event chains that can resolve, a lawful route through the central problem, and open play after resolution. Each feature can be modest in size while still having a complete beginning, consequence, and recovery path.

Depth should be added where the first game can support it well. More districts, more professions, deeper home ownership, extra vehicle types, longer relationship stories, richer romance, larger traffic systems, and more elaborate interior layouts are natural extensions. These should not be used to hide an unfinished core. A compact version where the people remember the player and the story can be completed is a better foundation for those additions. The document's examples should guide scope decisions rather than becoming an obligation to build every example at once.

Multiplayer is something I would like to explore, but it has not been confirmed as a required part of the first release. The design should avoid promising it until the actual scope is decided. Stable world identifiers, explicit transactions, and clear event ownership will help if shared play is added later. Multiplayer would still require decisions about who owns the world, how time and saves work, how crime and trust are assigned to each player, and how disconnections are handled. Those decisions need their own seperate implementation and testing.

A first-release test should begin from a fresh world and play through the ordinary entry points. The player should be able to finish a lawful shift, buy a recovery item, use it, make a friend, gain and lose that person's trust for understandable reasons, discover part of the protagonist's fixed history, accept or refuse an illegal opportunity, and reach a story resolution. They should then continue playing. A tester should be able to describe what caused each important change without needing to look into the code or anything.

The police tests should include an unseen act, an act noticed by someone who cannot identify the player, and an act clearly witnessed by someone who knows them. Each should create the appropriate amount of knowledge. A blue investigation should stay separate from immediate pursuit, and red pursuit should have a clear reason to begin. Losing direct contact should change the local response without deleting an identified incident. Resolving the case should update the relevant people and obligations once.

For a world and save test I'd start two new games and find the same clinic, shops, routes and homes in both. Then let time run in each without touching anything. People should go about their day and the incidents should be able to differ. Save one during an ambulance run, a pending witness call or a delivery and load it again. See whether it carries on with the same people, money, cargo and consequences. I also want to know the player can still get somewhere safe with an older or damaged save.

The presentation tests should cover the normal browser frame, fullscreen, a smaller laptop window, muted sound, keyboard navigation, and a return from another tab. Menus should not hide essential information or trigger world actions behind them. Pixel art should stay crisp, text should remain readable, and visible controls should match the actions they perform. The final exported ZIP needs to start from index.html and include the files required to play. An itch.io draft should be checked before calling the browser release ready.

I want the finished game to make small decisions feel connected. The player takes a shift because they need a repair, meets someone there, chooses whether to keep a promise, and later finds that the person remembers. A bad delivery creates pressure, but the player can look for another way out. A police incident changes the next day instead of ending the entire save. The city keeps its ordinary life around all of this. If those connections work, the humour, story, crime, friendships, and recovery will feel like parts of the same game.
